# Death salience and fear of death: prior art for calibration

The empirical literature behind a simple lifespan model of death anxiety (dread) and the practices that change it. Quality labels: **strong** means meta-analytic or large preregistered work. **moderate** means replicated but small or heterogeneous. **weak** means a single study, small N, or cross-sectional only. Effect sizes are approximate. If a source page did not give a number, none is given here.

## 1. Death anxiety across the lifespan

- **Peaks in young adulthood and declines, with a midlife bump for women.** On the Collett-Lester Fear of Death Scale, death anxiety peaked in the 20s for both sexes. Women showed a second rise around the early 50s. Both sexes were low and stable after about 60. *Quality: moderate (one cross-sectional sample; cohort is confounded with age).* Russac, Gatliff, Reece & Spottswood (2007), *Death Studies* 31(6). Secondary summary: https://www.wikiwand.com/en/articles/Fear_of_death
- **Older adults fear death less and accept it more.** In a comparison of 50 young, 50 middle-aged and 50 elderly adults, the elderly group scored lowest on fear of death and highest on all three kinds of death acceptance. *Quality: weak to moderate (small, cross-sectional).* Gesser, Wong & Reker (1988), *Omega* 18(2). https://scilit.com/publications/a8d338db60fca404832760e608e23d9b
- **In older adults, health and ego integrity predict dread more than age does.** Across 49 studies, higher death anxiety tracked lower ego integrity and more physical and psychological problems. Evidence on institutionalization was equivocal. *Quality: moderate (meta-analytic but old).* Fortner & Neimeyer (1999), *Death Studies* 23(5). https://www.scilit.com/publications/0f991ae32c882399c2a42c7d0401b6d8
- **Sex difference.** Women score higher on death anxiety scales fairly consistently. The gap is small to moderate and may partly reflect willingness to report fear. *Quality: moderate.* (Same Russac source as above.)

## 2. Terror management theory (TMT) and mortality salience (MS)

- **Original meta-analysis reported a medium effect.** Across 277 experiments, MS produced r ≈ .35 on worldview and self-esteem defense. The effect was larger with American and student samples and with a **longer delay** between the death prime and the outcome measure. *Quality: now doubtful (publication bias was likely).* Burke, Martens & Faucher (2010), *Personality and Social Psychology Review*. https://www.scilit.net/publications/27daf4efecb792fbe2eec04ecab67a64
- **Large replication failed.** Many Labs 4 ran 21 labs with more than 2,200 participants. It found little or no MS effect on worldview defense, whether or not original authors advised on the protocol. A Bayesian multiverse reanalysis also favored a null result. *Quality: strong evidence that the classic distal effect is small or absent.* Klein et al. (2022), *Collabra: Psychology* 8(1). https://cos.io/blog/many-labs-4-failure-replicate-mortality-salience-effect-and-without-original-author-involvement/ and https://forrt.org/flora-replication-atlas/doi/10.1525/collabra.35271/
- **Proximal vs distal defenses, with a delayed rise in death thoughts.** In the dual-process account, conscious death thoughts are first pushed away (proximal defense: suppression and distraction). Death-thought accessibility (DTA) then **rises after a delay**, which triggers distal worldview defense. Arndt et al. found the delayed DTA rise under low cognitive load. Under high load, which blocks suppression, the effects appeared immediately. Worldview defense then reduced the later DTA rise. *Quality: moderate in the 1990s; the downstream distal effects are now weakened by Many Labs 4.* Arndt, Greenberg, Pyszczynski, Solomon & Simon (1997), *JPSP* 73(1). https://experts.arizona.edu/en/publications/suppression-accessibility-of-death-related-thoughts-and-cultural--2/
- **General ironic rebound.** Suppressed thoughts come back more often later. A meta-analysis found a **small to moderate** rebound, with no evidence that clinical samples rebound more. *Quality: moderate.* Wegner et al. (1987), *JPSP*. Abramowitz, Tolin & Street (2001), *Clinical Psychology Review* 21(5). https://contextualscience.org/publications/paradoxical_effects_thought_suppression_metaanalysis_controlled_studies

## 3. Death reflection vs mortality salience

- **Death reflection reduced greed, while MS increased it.** Death reflection was a vivid scenario of dying in a fire plus life-review questions. Among highly extrinsic participants it reduced greed (raffle tickets taken) and raised self-reported spirituality. A standard MS prompt *increased* greed in the same group. The difference between death reflection and MS was significant, but death reflection versus control was only a trend. *Quality: weak (three small student studies, N = 48–90).* Cozzolino, Staples, Meyers & Samboceti (2004), *PSPB*. https://selfdeterminationtheory.org/SDT/documents/2004_CozzolinoStaplesMeyersSamoceti_PSPB.pdf
- **Vivid death imagery raised gratitude.** Vividly imagining one's own death raised state gratitude. Abstract writing about death (the MS-style condition) did not, and a daily-routine control fell slightly. The gain was short-term. *Quality: weak (single study).* Frias, Watkins, Webber & Froh (2011), *Journal of Positive Psychology*. https://greatergood.berkeley.edu/article/item/the_grateful_dead

## 4. Finitude, scarcity and savoring

- **Perceived scarcity increased engagement.** College seniors primed to see graduation as imminent took part in more college activities over two weeks than those primed to see it as distant. Higher well-being in that group is reported in the paper, but the press coverage did not confirm it. *Quality: weak (single study).* Kurtz (2008), *Psychological Science*. https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2009/01/090112110104.htm
- **"Last time" effect.** Chocolates labeled as "the last one" were rated more enjoyable and picked as the favorite 64% of the time, against 22% for the same position labeled "next". Participants also rated the whole tasting more positively. *Quality: weak (N = 52).* O'Brien & Ellsworth (2012), *Psychological Science*. https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/releases/what-kind-of-chocolate-is-best-the-last-you-taste-says-a-new-study.html
- **Socioemotional selectivity theory.** When time horizons shrink, goals shift from gathering information toward emotionally meaningful, present-focused experience. The theory says the driver is perceived time left, not age itself. *Quality: moderate to strong (many studies, including experiments that manipulate time horizons).* Carstensen and colleagues, for example Fung, Carstensen & Lutz (1999). https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socioemotional_selectivity_theory
- **Positivity effect in older adults.** Across 100 studies, the age difference in favor of positive over negative material was d ≈ 0.26 overall. It reached d ≈ 0.48 when attention was unconstrained and was ≈ 0.13 when constrained. *Quality: strong (meta-analysis); not universal across cultures.* Reed, Chan & Mikels (2014), *Psychology and Aging* 29(1). https://emocoglab.depaul.edu/publications/journals/Reed_Chan&Mikels_2014.pdf

## 5. Interventions

- **CBT and exposure work best.** A meta-analysis of 15 RCTs found an overall g ≈ 0.45. CBT, typically graded exposure or systematic desensitization, was large (g ≈ 1.7, though based on few and mostly low-quality trials). Other therapies were small (g ≈ 0.20). More sessions and higher baseline anxiety predicted larger effects. *Quality: moderate (low-quality primary studies, mostly nonclinical).* Menzies, Zuccala, Sharpe & Dar-Nimrod (2018), *Journal of Anxiety Disorders* 59. https://searchworks.jioinstitute.edu.in/articles/asx__132970609
- **Online CBT pilot.** A single-arm online CBT program had 10 completers. Of these, 60% showed reliable improvement in total death fear, and none got reliably worse. There was no follow-up and no distress data during exposure. *Quality: weak.* Menzies et al. (2023), *Behavioural and Cognitive Psychotherapy*. https://opus.lib.uts.edu.au/bitstream/10453/169802/2/overcoming-death-anxiety-a-phase-i-trial-of-an-online-cbt-program-in-a-clinical-sample.pdf
- **Exposure logic.** Exposure protocols use graded imaginal and in-vivo tasks: writing one's own death story or eulogy, visiting cemeteries, making a will, reading obituaries. The death-anxiety studies found here **did not report** whether distress rises at first. A temporary rise in distress during exposure is the standard expectation from the general anxiety-exposure literature (inferred, not measured here). Clinical overview: Menzies & Menzies (2018), *InPsych*. https://psychology.org.au/for-members/Publications/inpsych/2018/December-Issue-6/Death-anxiety-The-worm-at-the-core-of-mental-heal
- **General mindfulness vs contemplation.** In a 6-week RCT (N = 89 students), mindfulness that never mentioned death and a contemplation control **both** produced large drops in fear of one's own dying (partial η² ≈ 0.54 for time), with no difference between groups. A short-term rise in fear of others dying appeared right after the intervention. *Quality: weak (no inactive control).* Mindfulness (Springer), 2022. https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12671-022-01967-8
- **Buddhist death meditation (maranasati).** The evidence is mostly cross-sectional or small. Among older Thai meditators, positive death attitudes correlated with well-being (r ≈ .39). *Quality: weak.* Glushich et al. (2025), *Frontiers in Public Health*. https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpubh.2025.1622813/full
- **Meaning-centered group psychotherapy.** In 253 patients with advanced cancer, eight sessions beat supportive group therapy on spiritual well-being, meaning and quality of life. It also reduced hopelessness, depression and desire for hastened death. **Anxiety did not differ** between groups. Gains held at 2 months. *Quality: moderate (one large RCT).* Breitbart et al. (2015), *Journal of Clinical Oncology*. https://medicalxpress.com/news/2015-02-meaning-centered-group-therapy-beneficial-advanced.pdf
- **Psilocybin for patients with cancer.** Griffiths (N = 51) reported large, lasting cuts in depression and anxiety, with about 80% still improved at 6 months and lower death anxiety. Ross (N = 29) found large effects on anxiety and depression and 60–80% responders at 6.5 months. However, on its formal Death Anxiety Scale, Ross found **no significant change**, and only death-transcendence scores improved. Follow-up at 3.2 and 4.5 years kept 60–80% responders. *Quality: moderate (small trials, blinding hard to maintain).* Griffiths et al. (2016) and Ross et al. (2016), *Journal of Psychopharmacology*; Agin-Liebes et al. (2020), same journal. https://hub.jhu.edu/2016/12/01/hallucinogen-treats-cancer-depression-anxiety/, https://www.Gwern.net/doc/psychedelic/2016-ross.pdf, https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2020/01/200128115423.htm

## 6. Buffers: religiosity and meaning

- **Religiosity has an inverted-U relation to death anxiety.** The meta-analysis covered about 100 studies and roughly 26,000 people. Of the 11 studies that could test for a curve, 10 found that both the very religious and the confidently nonreligious have *lower* death anxiety than those in between. The overall linear relation is weak. *Quality: moderate.* Jong et al. (2018), *Religion, Brain & Behavior*. https://www.ox.ac.uk/node/1225441
- **Meaning and ego integrity.** Meaning and ego integrity relate to lower death anxiety in older adults (Fortner & Neimeyer, 1999, above). Meaning-centered therapy improves meaning but not anxiety itself (Breitbart, above). Together these suggest meaning buffers despair more than fear.

## 7. Daily impact and prevalence

- **Death anxiety is transdiagnostic and moderately correlated with distress.** A 2025 meta-analysis pooled 129 studies with more than 34,000 people. Death anxiety correlated with distress at r ≈ .36, with anxiety at .42, depression at .41 and OCD symptoms at .66. The correlation was stronger with a medical illness present (r ≈ .48). Sex had no moderating effect. Most studies were cross-sectional and of poor quality, so the direction of cause is unknown. Dumitru et al. (2025), *Nature Human Behaviour* 9. https://www.nature.com/articles/s41562-025-02115-7
- **Prevalence.** Clinically relevant death anxiety appears in about a third of patients with advanced cancer, according to Neel et al. (2015) as cited in Lu et al. (2024), *Frontiers in Psychology*. https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2024.1362127/full No good general-population estimate of clinically meaningful death anxiety was found. Most healthy people report moderate scale scores without impairment.

## Calibration implications

a. **Dread vs age.** Dread should *fall* with age after a peak in the 20s, with an optional midlife bump for women around age 50. Model dread as driven by perceived threat and low meaning or integrity, plus poor health, rather than by age itself. Late-life dread rises mainly with illness.

b. **Targeted practice vs general equanimity.** For dread itself, targeted exposure-style CBT (g ≈ 1.7, low-quality trials) beats generic therapy (g ≈ 0.2). One RCT found general mindfulness matched a contemplation control. Meaning-based work raises meaning but not anxiety. Give targeted practice a larger effect on dread, and give general practices modest, nonspecific effects.

c. **Suppression.** Suppression keeps acute death thoughts low at first and then rebounds after a delay (Arndt 1997; rebound small to moderate). The downstream defensive effects of MS replicate poorly (Many Labs 4). Model a small delayed rebound, not a large effect.

d. **Finitude and savoring.** Finitude awareness boosts savoring and appreciation, but only in a reflective or vivid framing (death reflection, "last time", shrinking time horizons). Abstract MS does not do this. The effects are small to moderate and short-lived, while the shift of SST and the positivity effect with age is the steadier one.

e. **Short-term cost of exposure.** Exposure probably carries a short-term distress cost, by analogy with exposure therapy in general (not directly measured for death anxiety). Model a transient rise in dread followed by a decline below baseline.
