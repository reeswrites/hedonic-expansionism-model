Date: 2026-11-25. This complements `prior_art.md` (habituation, Berlyne, incentive sensitization, opponent process, stress and reward, expertise numbing, compassion vs. empathy). Those topics are not repeated here.

## 1. Evolutionary reasons for hedonic adaptation and reference-dependent utility

| Work | Verdict | What it says / implies |
|---|---|---|
| Robson, A. J. (2001). "The Biological Basis of Economic Behavior." *J. Economic Literature* 39(1):11-33. https://ideas.repec.org/a/aea/jeclit/v39y2001i1p11-33.html [verified; sec. 2.1.1 read via https://legacy.econ.tuwien.ac.at/hanappi/AgeSo/rp/robson_2001.pdf] | **Supports** time decay; **complicates** expansion | Adaptation is advantageous because organisms have "limited discriminatory power." Like a voltmeter, utility must be scaled to the range of options met. With one threshold the optimal cutoff is the median of the distribution, so "it is optimal for utility to adapt to the distribution F." The paper also says the evolutionary rationale for utility is "to deal with novelty and complexity." **Implication:** adaptation is *range-setting*, not decay of a fixed stock. Adding sources shifts the scale, so expansion could partly be eaten by recalibration. |
| Rayo, L. & Becker, G. S. (2007). "Evolutionary Efficiency and Happiness." *J. Political Economy* 115(2):302-337. https://ideas.repec.org/a/ucp/jpolec/v115y2007p302-337.html; working paper https://sticerd.lse.ac.uk/dps/bpde2004/Rayo.pdf [verified] | **Supports** decay and recovery; **complicates** long-run gains | Happiness is a bounded incentive tool (V in [0,1]) designed by selection. The optimal reference point is the conditional expectation of output, so habits (reference = last period's output) and peer comparison fall out as special cases. Happiness is mean-reverting: "its expected value is the same every period." Novelty and variety are not addressed. **Implication:** a model that lets average hedonic level rise without limit through expansion conflicts with this framework, unless expansion works by raising the *share of time near the top of a bounded range* (more fresh, positively surprising sources), not by raising the ceiling. The model's "ceiling per moment, routes unbounded" result (P2) is compatible. |
| Netzer, N. (2009). "Evolution of Time Preferences and Attitudes toward Risk." *American Economic Review* 99(3):937-955. https://ideas.repec.org/a/aea/aecrev/v99y2009i3p937-55.html [verified, abstract] | **Supports** resolution growing with engagement | Optimal hedonic utility is "steeper where choices are frequent and decision mistakes are costly for fitness," and this yields S-shaped value functions that adapt to the environment. **Implication:** hedonic *sensitivity* (the model's resolution) should concentrate where an agent often chooses. This gives an evolutionary rationale for "engagement raises resolution in a domain," but it is a reallocation of a bounded sensitivity budget. It implies a cost: resolution gained in one domain may be lost elsewhere. |
| Perez-Truglia, R. (2012). "On the causes and consequences of hedonic adaptation." *J. Economic Psychology* 33(6):1182-1192. https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/joepsy/v33y2012i6p1182-1192.html [verified, abstract] | **Supports** asymmetric adaptation | Sensations with "warning and defense roles" (such as pain) should not adapt, while others should. This contrasts with Robson and Rayo & Becker. **Implication:** suffering should decay/habituate more slowly than pleasure. That is a second, evolutionarily grounded source of asymmetry, separate from lambda. |
| Diener, E., Lucas, R. E. & Scollon, C. N. (2006). "Beyond the hedonic treadmill: Revising the adaptation theory of well-being." *American Psychologist* 61(4):305-314. https://ink.library.smu.edu.sg/soss_research/921 [verified abstract; vol/pages from memory] | **Supports** that expansion can matter | Five revisions: set points are not neutral; they differ by person; there are several per person; "well-being set points can change under some conditions"; and people differ in adaptation. This is the empirical license for a model where long-run level is movable at all. |

**Does evolution predict the model's time decay, recovery, and value of breadth/novelty?** It predicts decay and recovery (reference updating, Rayo & Becker; range-setting, Robson). Robson explicitly ties utility's purpose to novelty and complexity. None of these papers derives a *value of breadth*. Their logic actually raises the main threat: the reference point catches up with whatever you add.

**Model implications.** (a) Add a slow *reference/aspiration level* that tracks recent average pleasure (this matches the aspiration-creep suggestion in `prior_art.md`). Freshness then measures surprise relative to it, and gains from expansion show up as time spent above the reference, not as permanent level shifts. (b) Make adaptation rate valence-dependent: rho_pain < rho_pleasure (Perez-Truglia). (c) Treat resolution as a bounded budget allocated by choice frequency (Netzer).

## 2. Negativity bias and loss aversion as evolved asymmetries (lambda > 1)

| Work | Verdict | Notes / parameters |
|---|---|---|
| Baumeister, R. F., Bratslavsky, E., Finkenauer, C. & Vohs, K. D. (2001). "Bad is stronger than good." *Review of General Psychology* 5(4):323-370. https://fbaum.unc.edu/teaching/articles/Baumeister_2001.pdf [verified, full text] | **Supports** (descriptive) | "Hardly any exceptions (indicating greater power of good) can be found." Evolutionary rationale: "Survival requires urgent attention to possible bad outcomes, but it is less urgent with regard to good ones." The only explicit offset ratio is Gottman's "at least five to one" positive:negative interactions in marriages. Other ratios: aversive behaviors explained 65% vs. 25% of variance in marital satisfaction (Wills et al. 1974), and recalled emotional events were about 4:1 bad:good. Noted exceptions: social support sometimes outweighs conflict (Major et al. 1997), and good and bad relations were about equal for broad social networks (Abbey et al. 1985). |
| Rozin, P. & Royzman, E. B. (2001). "Negativity bias, negativity dominance, and contagion." *Personality and Social Psychology Review* 5(4):296-320. https://pdodds.w3.uvm.edu/files/papers/others/2001/rozin2001a.pdf [verified, full text; vol/pages from memory] | **Supports**, and gives a structure | Four components: negative potency, steeper negative gradients, negativity dominance (combinations are worse than the sum), and **negative differentiation** (negative states are more elaborated, with 31 pain words vs. 14 pleasure words). Also the **positivity offset** (Cacioppo): at low intensity, positive is stronger; negativity has a steeper slope but a lower intercept. |
| Brown, A. L., Imai, T., Vieider, F. & Camerer, C. (2024). "Meta-analysis of Empirical Estimates of Loss Aversion." *J. Economic Literature* 62(2):485-516. Preprint: https://ideas.repec.org/p/osf/metaar/hnefr.html; JEL record https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:aea:jeclit:v:62:y:2024:i:2:p:485-516 [verified abstract] | **Supports lambda around 2 as a decision weight** | 607 estimates from 150 articles: "the mean loss aversion coefficient is between 1.8 and 2.1." Estimates vary with design. |
| Gal, D. & Rucker, D. D. (2018). "The Loss of Loss Aversion: Will It Loom Larger Than Its Gain?" *J. Consumer Psychology* 28(3):497-516. https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=3049660 [verified abstract; vol/pages from memory] | **Complicates** | "Current evidence does not support that losses, on balance, tend to be any more impactful than gains." They argue for a contextual view. |
| Kermer, D. A., Driver-Linn, E., Wilson, T. D. & Gilbert, D. T. (2006). "Loss aversion is an affective forecasting error." *Psychological Science* 17(8):649-653. https://www.psychologicalscience.org/journals/psychological-science/j.1467-9280.2006.01760.x/ [verified abstract and volume] | **Complicates** | People predict losses will hurt more than equal gains please, but during actual gambling losses stung less than expected. The asymmetry is larger in *forecasts* than in *experience*. |
| McDermott, R., Fowler, J. H. & Smirnov, O. (2008). "On the evolutionary origin of prospect theory preferences." *J. Politics* 70(2):335-350. https://researchconnect.stonybrook.edu/en/publications/on-the-evolutionary-origin-of-prospect-theory-preferences/ [verified abstract] | **Supports** (an evolutionary model) | Derives context-dependent risk attitudes from risk-sensitive optimal foraging. |

**Model implications.** The most important point is that the model's lambda is a **normative** weight ("suffering weighted more"), while all the evidence above is **descriptive** (decision weights or felt intensity).
- (i) Evolutionary negativity bias explains why suffering *feels* intense. If experienced suffering already carries that intensity, multiplying by lambda = 2 again **double counts**, unless the model's suffering inputs are in objective units. State which units are used.
- (ii) The descriptive data put lambda around 2 for decisions (Brown et al.) but closer to 1 for experience (Kermer; Gal & Rucker). So lambda = 2 is defensible only as an ethical choice, and the model should sweep it (1 to 5) rather than cite loss aversion as calibration.
- (iii) Rozin & Royzman's **positivity offset** suggests suffering weight is intensity-dependent: about 1 or below at low intensity, rising steeply at high intensity. A convex suffering function would replace the constant lambda, and this would also strengthen the model's "relief share rises with distress" result (P4).
- (iv) **Negative differentiation** is directly relevant to fractal resolution. The evolved mind already has finer resolution for bad states, so at baseline, raising resolution may reveal aversive features faster than likable ones. This supports the model's "capture" caveat.

## 3. Wanting vs. liking in evolutionary terms; mismatch accounts of addiction

| Work | Verdict | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Nesse, R. M. & Berridge, K. C. (1997). "Psychoactive drug use in evolutionary perspective." *Science* 278(5335):63-66. https://scilit.com/publications/98e93720c1ee43231a9a90c34da2d0b8 [verified abstract summary] | **Supports** | Pure drugs and direct routes are evolutionarily novel. They bypass adaptive information processing and give a "false signal of fitness benefit," hijacking "liking" and "wanting" and sometimes sustaining use after pleasure is gone. Drugs that block negative emotion can disable useful defenses. |
| Berridge, K. C. & Robinson, T. E. (2016). "Liking, wanting, and the incentive-sensitization theory of addiction." *American Psychologist* 71(8):670-679. https://moodle2.units.it/pluginfile.php/590619/mod_resource/content/0/BerridgeRobinson2016.pdf [verified, full text] | **Supports** the asymmetry of the two systems | Wanting comes from "large and robust neural systems." Liking comes from tiny hotspots (about 1 mm³ in rats) that are "easily disrupted." The authors suggest this is "perhaps a reason why intense pleasures are relatively few and far between in life compared with intense desires." Sensitization is "very long lasting, and possibly even permanent." The paper gives **no explicit evolutionary rationale** for the split. |
| McClure, S. M., Daw, N. D. & Montague, P. R. (2003). "A computational substrate for incentive salience." *Trends in Neurosciences* 26(8):423-428. https://collaborate.princeton.edu/en/publications/a-computational-substrate-for-incentive-salience/ [verified abstract] | **Supports** "wanting = learning/motivation signal" | Reconciles dopamine-as-prediction-error with dopamine-as-incentive in one TD model. Wanting is the learned value that drives action selection, so it serves learning and pursuit, not experience. That is the functional reason to keep it separate from liking. |
| Hagen, E. H., Roulette, C. J. & Sullivan, R. J. (2013). "Explaining human recreational use of 'pesticides': the neurotoxin regulation model of substance use vs. the hijack model..." *Frontiers in Psychiatry* 4:142. https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyt.2013.00142/full [verified] | **Complicates** the mismatch account | Most drugs are plant toxins that "evolved to deter, not reward or reinforce, consumption." In choice studies, most animals pick a rewarding alternative (sweet water, social contact) over the drug. The authors propose regulated neurotoxin intake rather than passive hijack. |
| Lenoir, M., Serre, F., Cantin, L. & Ahmed, S. H. (2007). "Intense sweetness surpasses cocaine reward." *PLoS ONE* 2(8):e698. https://www.bordeaux-neurocampus.fr/?p=89218 [verified summary] | **Supports** breadth as protection | 94% of rats preferred saccharin to IV cocaine, including drug-sensitized rats and rats escalating intake. |
| Venniro, M. et al. (2018). "Volitional social interaction prevents drug addiction in rat models." *Nature Neuroscience* 21(11):1520-1529. https://irp.nih.gov/accomplishments/rewarding-social-interaction-prevents-drug-addiction-in-rats [verified summary; percentages not read] | **Supports** breadth and social relief as protection | Rats strongly preferred social interaction over opioids or psychostimulants, and showed less craving in abstinence. |

**Model implications.** The split is evolutionarily sensible: wanting is an anticipatory learning/selection signal (McClure et al.), and in ancestral environments it tracked liking well enough. The mismatch view predicts that the wanting-liking *gap* is small for ancestral-type rewards and large for evolutionarily novel, concentrated ones. The model should therefore make the gap (or the sensitization gain kappa in the Zhang et al. form) **source-specific**, with a "novel/concentrated" flag, rather than a single global value. The choice data (Lenoir; Venniro; Hagen's critique) support a testable prediction: **the probability of lock-in falls as the value of available alternatives rises.** That links breadth to protection from capture, a link the model can prove or simulate.

**Model implications.** This is the best-grounded part of the model. Acquired taste = learned safety (Zajonc) + perceptual learning, minus boredom (Berlyne, already in `prior_art.md`).
- **Parameters:** about 5-15 spaced exposures to acquire a food taste for typical children, with a long tail to about 27 (Keller). This matches the single-digit peaks from Szpunar et al. for music.
- Add a **per-person neophobia cost** on the first k exposures (heritable, so a fixed trait) and an **abandonment hazard**: an agent that quits before threshold never acquires the taste. This is the same "never sampled, never learned" mechanism as Delaney et al. in `prior_art.md`.
- Benign masochism supports adding a class of **reversible-aversive sources**, whose a-process is negative but whose value turns positive once threat appraisal is removed. That is a direct link from equanimity to expansion, and Rozin gives the boundary: real-harm signals (nausea, visceral pain) don't reverse.

## 5. Curiosity, complexity, learning progress, predictive processing

**Does any theory predict that complex sources renew longer?** Schmidhuber's theory does, qualitatively (interest lasts while learnable regularity remains). LP theories imply it (a sequence of ever-more-complex niches), but their authors don't claim it. Szpunar (from `prior_art.md`) gives weak empirical support. Madison & Schiölde suggest that, with spacing, even simple music keeps rising over 28 exposures, so complexity may matter less than spacing.

**Model implications.**
- Replace a fixed freshness-recovery rule for skill-building sources with a **learning-progress reward**: pleasure is proportional to the decrease in prediction error, with total learnable structure C_s per source. Then "complex sources renew longer" becomes a derived result, not an assumption.
- Expansion then includes **domain-level transfer**. Madison & Schiölde's style-familiarity effect suggests learning a genre raises liking of unheard pieces in it, which is a cleaner version of "fractal" expansion than within-item resolution.
- The two-variable pleasure surface (Cheung et al.) suggests freshness should be split into uncertainty and surprise.

**Model implications.**
- Shared relief should be modeled as a **reduction in the cost of threat/effort** (Coan), i.e., a multiplier on suffering input, not added pleasure. This is the strongest grounding for the model's finding that close, chosen company wins "mainly through shared relief."
- Relief from a tie = stranger baseline + quality term (Coan 2006).
- Dunbar supports a cap of about 5 on the inner layer, a time share of about 40%, and **contact-dependent decay of closeness** (ties need upkeep, family excepted). The model's optimum of 2-4 sits inside Dunbar's inner layer.
- Autonomy should enter as a sign-flipping moderator of both company and solitude (Nguyen et al.; Weinstein & Ryan), not just a small bonus.
- Woo et al. caution against treating social and physical suffering as interchangeable inputs with one lambda.

**Model implications.**
- Attentional capture by threat is evolved but **expressed mainly under anxiety** (Bar-Haim), so make capture a function of current distress. This ties the "fractal" result (resolution pays only without capture) to the bottleneck.
- For the "labels dampen affect both ways" claim, the evidence is weaker than the model needs. Affect labeling clearly dampens negative affect. - Recast the model's "concept release" term as **explanation release**: keeping some uncertainty prolongs positive affect, and explaining shortens negative affect. **"Pleasure is a fitness signal, not a goal": how hedonists answer.**
- (1) **The genetic fallacy / functional reply.** Why a capacity exists doesn't settle what it is worth now. We value vision's deliverances without valuing reproductive success. Hedonism assigns value to the experienced quality, not to its function [standard reply; memory].
- (2) **Introspective access** (Sinhababu): the goodness is read off the experience by a reliable process, so it is not an inferred belief exposed to debunking.
- (3) **The motivation-is-enough argument** (LRS via Jaquet): selection had no need to install a *belief* that pleasure is good. Its weakness is the by-product route.
- (4) **The subjectivist retreat** (Jaquet): attitudinal or subjective hedonism is immune to debunking but gives up hedonism's objectivity.

For hedonic expansionism, the signal view cuts *both ways*. If pleasure is a calibrated signal, then deliberately expanding liking decouples it from fitness, which the debunker sees as "hacking the gauge." But that decoupling is exactly what a hedonist who values experience over fitness should want, so long as it does not also disable the warning functions of suffering (Perez-Truglia; Nesse & Berridge on blocking negative emotions).

**Model implications.**
- The model is a normative model with descriptive dynamics. State this explicitly. Its welfare function (pleasure minus lambda times suffering) needs no evolutionary justification. Its dynamics (adaptation, wanting, capture, neophobia) should be evolutionarily realistic, and they mostly are.
- Add a **guardrail**: expansion interventions should not lower the *informational* function of suffering. For example, numbing that disables warning signals carries an expected-harm cost.
- Lambda should be treated as an ethical parameter justified philosophically (for example by priority to the worse-off, or the "suffering is more urgent" intuition, which is itself debunkable), not by loss aversion.

## Overall verdict

**Evolution and cognition support the model's dynamics better than its headline.**
- **Well grounded:** decay and recovery (Rayo & Becker; Robson); acquired taste through safe, spaced exposure (Zajonc; Wardle; Keller; Rozin's benign masochism); curiosity favoring learnable complexity (Schmidhuber; LP theories; Van de Cruys); separate wanting and liking, with mismatch-driven gaps (Nesse & Berridge; McClure et al.); social relief as lowered threat cost (Coan & Sbarra), with a small inner circle (Dunbar); autonomy mattering for company and solitude (Nguyen et al.; Weinstein & Ryan); evolved, state-dependent threat capture (Öhman; Bar-Haim); explanation numbing positive affect (Wilson & Gilbert).
- **Weak or contested:** "labels dampen both ways" (positive-affect evidence is thin and partly contrary); "social pain = physical pain" (Woo et al.); lambda calibrated from loss aversion (Gal & Rucker; Kermer).
- **The strongest objection is evolutionary, not philosophical.** Reference-dependent, range-setting hedonics (Robson; Rayo & Becker) imply that expanding what you like gets **recalibrated away**: the reference point rises and mean happiness reverts. **Best reply:** the model already concedes a per-moment ceiling. Under bounded, reference-dependent utility, expansion pays through (a) more time above a moving reference, because more independent fresh sources supply more positive surprises; (b) learning-progress pleasure that renews as long as learnable structure remains (Schmidhuber); (c) resilience when a source is lost; and (d) set points being movable (Diener et al. 2006). Pain adapting less than pleasure (Perez-Truglia) makes suffering-reduction the more durable gain, consistent with the model's relief-first results. The reply is testable: if expansion only shifts the reference, logged experience-sampled affect should rise transiently and then revert.

## Suggested model changes

1. **Moving reference level.** Add r_t that tracks recent average pleasure at a slow rate. Score momentary pleasure partly relative to it (Rayo & Becker; Robson; Sheldon & Lyubomirsky's aspiration creep). Then re-test P2 and the expansion results: do gains survive as time-above-reference?
2. **Valence-specific adaptation and intensity-dependent suffering weight.** Set rho_pain < rho_pleasure (Perez-Truglia). Replace the constant lambda with a convex w(s) that is about 1 at low intensity and above 2 at high intensity (positivity offset; steeper negative gradients). Sweep lambda from 1 to 5, label it normative, and remove any appeal to loss aversion as calibration.
3. **Learning-progress pleasure for skill-building sources.** Pleasure is proportional to the drop in prediction error, with learnable structure C_s per source plus style-level transfer (Schmidhuber; Van de Cruys; Madison & Schiölde). "Complex sources renew longer" then becomes derived, not assumed.
4. **Source-specific wanting-liking gap and alternatives-dependent lock-in.** Flag evolutionarily novel or concentrated sources with high kappa. Make lock-in probability fall with the value of available alternatives, including social relief (Nesse & Berridge; Lenoir et al.; Venniro et al.).
5. **Distress-dependent capture and asymmetric explanation.** Let capture rise with current distress (Bar-Haim). Split "concept release" into explain-suffering (dampens negative) and leave-pleasure-unexplained (prolongs positive) (Wilson & Gilbert). Add a per-person neophobia cost and abandonment hazard to acquired taste (Keller; neophobia heritability [memory]).
